The honest framing in the source comments is correct; Sentinel adds that the *practical* strength of this mitigation depends on grant volume and should not be oversold as anonymity in a small circle.
  - Reconciliation is explicitly **by count, never by per-entry linkage** against the jar's on-chain `granted` counter (`faucetLedger.ts:22-23`), which avoids the treasurer (or an adversary who compromises the treasurer's key) building a confident entry↔transaction map even with the timing side-channel above, beyond the small-circle correlation risk just described.

So the "amounts" half of the round brief's combined timing+amount correlation check is sound; the "timing" half is honestly-acknowledged-but-imperfect as described above.

## Verdict rationale

**Plain language, for a trusted servant who does not read code.**

This round the team built two real, meaningful pieces of privacy engineering, and I want to say clearly that both work as intended. First, there is now a way for one member to vouch for a newcomer without their name ever being written down anywhere on the public ledger — I checked the account that gets created when someone vouches this new, anonymous way, and it genuinely contains nothing that points back to them; even the two accompanying files knitted together give no thread to pull. This is the first time since I started checking this project that a sponsor relationship has actually been *removed* from the record, rather than just documented as a known problem. Second, the "first gas" faucet from last round now has the encrypted ledger it was missing: the treasurer can see a list of one-time codes and amounts, sealed so only they can open it, and the file that holds it contains nothing else — no name, no wallet, no transaction ID. Both pieces come with their own honest fine print about their limits, which the team wrote themselves rather than me having to find it, and in both cases I checked that fine print against the code and found it accurate rather than overstated.

Here is why the answer is still a fail despite that real progress. The new anonymous vouching only removes the sponsor's name from the *account* it creates — the *transaction* that submits it is still paid for and signed by the sponsor's own wallet, because the "someone else pays so you can't be traced" piece (a relayer) hasn't been built yet. The team's own code comments say this outright, so nobody is hiding it, but it means that today, in practice, a careful observer of the blockchain's full history could still figure out who vouched for whom by watching who paid the fee — the new privacy protection is real for someone looking only at today's account snapshot, but not yet real for someone looking at the permanent transaction history, which on a public blockchain is everyone, forever. Meanwhile, the three problems I found in the very first check I ever ran on this project are all still there and untouched by this round's work: a Circle's full member list and every member's wallet address are still openly readable by anyone; the faucet's actual money-moving transaction still puts the sponsor's wallet and the newcomer's wallet side by side in public, in one visible payment; and private messages still leak who is talking to whom and when, even though their contents stay secret. None of that is new — it's the same three findings from before, still open — but our own rule says even one of these counts as the most serious kind of problem there is, so the round cannot pass while they remain.

I also have to flag something that got worse on paper, though it's really a case of a promise not yet kept rather than a new mistake. Last time, I found that members earn visible "chips" and a visible skill level that anyone can see and use to rank members against each other, and I asked the team to either write down why that's acceptable or plan to remove it. Nobody wrote that justification down — I checked, and so did the team's own project notes, which reached the same conclusion I did. Ranking members against each other is one of the specific things this project's founding principle explicitly forbids, so with no explanation offered, I'm treating this as a serious problem this time rather than a friendly reminder.

To be fair and complete: nothing I checked this round showed a step backward. The math behind the anonymous proofs is still exactly as sound as it was last time, byte for byte. The email notification system that I flagged as wide open last round is still properly locked down. No tracking software, no scoring field with a forbidden name, and no logging of private information crept in anywhere in this round's new code. The team is visibly working through the list I gave them, in the right order, and being honest in their own code and project notes about exactly what's fixed and what isn't — that matters, and I want it on record. But the rulebook is not graded on effort, it's graded on whether the promises hold today, and today they still don't, so the verdict is FAIL again, for the same underlying reasons as before plus one that has now had its warning period expire.
